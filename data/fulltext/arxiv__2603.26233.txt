Ask or Assume? Uncertainty-Aware Clarification-Seeking in Coding Agents
Nicholas Edwards1,2 Sebastian Schuster1
1Faculty of Computer Science, University of Vienna, Vienna, Austria
2UniVie Doctoral School Computer Science, University of Vienna, Vienna, Austria
{nicholas.edwards, sebastian.schuster}@univie.ac.at
Abstract
As Large Language Model (LLM) agents are
increasingly deployed in open-ended domains
like software engineering, they frequently en-
counterunderspecifiedinstructions that lack
crucial context. While human developers natu-
rally resolve underspecification by asking clari-
fying questions, current agents are largely opti-
mized for autonomous execution. In this work,
we systematically evaluate the clarification-
seeking abilities of LLM agents on an under-
specified variant of SWE-bench Verified. We
propose an uncertainty-aware multi-agent scaf-
fold that decouples underspecification detec-
tion from code execution. Across both pro-
prietary and open-weight frontier LLMs, our
scaffold achieves a 69.40% task resolve rate,
significantly outperforming a standard single-
agent setup and closing the performance gap
with agents operating on fully specified instruc-
tions. Furthermore, we find that the multi-agent
system exhibits well-calibrated information-
seeking behavior, conserving queries on simple
tasks while proactively seeking information on
more complex issues. These findings indicate
that current models can be turned into proactive
collaborators, where agents independently rec-
ognize when to ask questions to elicit missing
information in real-world, underspecified tasks.
1 Introduction
A core property of a good collaborator is the ability
to establish and maintain a shared understanding of
a goal (Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986). In human
communication, this is rarely achieved through a
single perfectly specified instruction; instead, inter-
locutors infer missing context and explicitly signal
uncertainty, such as by asking clarification ques-
tions (Clark, 1996; Hawkins et al., 2015).
While information gathering in response to un-
derspecification has been studied in the NLP do-
main, such as in question answering and conver-
sational search (Rao and Daumé III, 2018; Alian-
nejadi et al., 2019; Zhang and Choi, 2025), these
settings involve static or single-turn interactions
over bounded contexts.
In contrast, modern AI agents are increasingly
deployed in more naturalistic, open-ended domains
such as software engineering. For instance, fixing
real-world GitHub issues (Jimenez et al., 2024) re-
quires exploring, understanding, and editing large-
scale repositories often containing hundreds of files
and thousands of lines of code, while remaining
aligned with implicit developer intentions. Existing
agents are optimized for autonomous completion
rather than interactive collaboration, creating a crit-
ical gap between user intent and agent execution
(METR, 2025; Shen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026).
Given these constraints, we investigate the abili-
ties of LLMs to assess uncertainty and determine
how and when to seek information. Specifically, we
consider how models handleexternal uncertainty:
uncertainty arising from ambiguous or underspeci-
fied task inputs, as opposed to internal uncertainty
inherent to the model itself (Li et al., 2026b). While
LLMs can exhibit sensitivity to uncertainty (Kada-
vath et al., 2022), this is not always robust (An-
dukuri et al., 2024; Kapoor et al., 2024). Beyond
simply detecting external uncertainty, a true agentic
collaborator must be able to continuously monitor
for such uncertainty and proactively initiate dia-
logue to elicit missing information. We define such
a system asuncertainty-aware. Importantly, we
use this term in a behavioral rather than formalized
mathematical sense, evaluating how effectively an
agent modulates its information-seeking behavior
based on the severity of underspecification.
We systematically evaluate the clarification-
seeking abilities of LLM agents within a dynamic,
multi-turn software engineering framework. We
conduct our evaluation using an underspecified
variant of SWE-bench Verified (Chowdhury et al.,
2024; Vijayvargiya et al., 2026b), where informa-
tion is removed from the dataset’s original GitHub
issues. To assess the capabilities of agents to act

2 Related Work
Proactive Dialogue AgentsIn human commu-
nication, interlocutors use clarification requests to
establish shared understanding and resolve ambigu-
ity (Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986). Recent work
in the NLP domain has considered clarification-
seeking behavior in the context of dialogue-based
tasks like open-domain question-answering, con-
versational search and preference elicitation (Rao
and Daumé III, 2018; Aliannejadi et al., 2019; Kim
et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Zhang and Choi, 2025;
Zhang et al., 2025).
To elicit these proactive clarification-seeking be-
haviors, prior work has explored various method-
ological approaches. For instance, Zhang et al.
(2025) propose modeling the utility of future con-
versation trajectories to teach LLMs when a ques-
tion is necessary; Andukuri et al. (2024) utilize sim-
ulated self-play to iteratively improve the model’s
clarification skills; and Testoni and Fernández
(2024) introduce an uncertainty-guided approach,
demonstrating that leveraging a model’s own un-
certainty (operationalized as predictive entropy) to
trigger questions is more effective than imitating
human clarification-seeking behavior. While these
methods have proven effective in bounded conver-
sational settings, our work extends this paradigm
by evaluating how agents dynamically elicit miss-
ing information in open-ended, multi-step software
engineering environments.
Agentic Systems for Ambiguous Code Genera-
tionAs LLM agents are increasingly deployed
in software engineering, robustly handling ambigu-
ous or underspecified instructions presents a major
challenge. Existing coding environments used to
evaluate clarification-seeking behaviors often focus
on tasks targeting edits to isolated functions, typi-
cally allowing only a single, predetermined round
of clarification or short, constrained exchanges (Li
et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2024; Wu and Fard, 2025;
Li et al., 2026a).
To improve the clarification-seeking behavior
of agents in dynamic, multi-turn coding settings,
recent literature has introduced training pipelines
leveraging reinforcement learning (Sun et al., 2026)
and contrastive training (Chen et al., 2025) tech-
niques. However, these frameworks often impose
rigid interaction structures. For example, both Vi-
jayvargiya et al. (2026a) and Darji and Lutellier
(2025) strictly constrain question-asking to the very
beginning of the trajectory, deferring all code exe-
cution until the initial prompt is disambiguated.
In practice, relevant missing information may
only become apparent after initial exploration of a
codebase. Most closely related to our evaluation
setting, Vijayvargiya et al. (2026b) analyze how
LLM agents handle underspecification in software
engineering tasks. In our work, we demonstrate
that a specialized multi-agent scaffold that decou-
ples underspecification detection from code execu-
tion enables agents to navigate underspecified tasks
and actively ask questions throughout a trajectory.
Uncertainty Detection in LLMsA prerequisite
for proactive clarification is an agent’s ability to
accurately gauge its own uncertainty. Foundational
work demonstrates that base LLMs exhibit a degree
of internal calibration, enabling them to estimate
the likelihood of their correctness or express their
confidence in natural language (Kadavath et al.,
2022; Lin et al., 2022). However, this calibration is
not always robust out of the box (Kapoor et al.,
2024; Andukuri et al., 2024), as standard post-
training alignment techniques, such as RLHF, can
inadvertently degrade calibration and encourage
model overconfidence (Zhou et al., 2024). Further-
more, effective interaction requires distinguishing
between different sources of uncertainty. For in-
stance, recent work has highlighted the difference
between uncertainty caused by a model’s lack of
parametric knowledge versus input underspecifi-
cation or ambiguity (Hou et al., 2024; Zhang and

Choi, 2025). Formally quantifying this distinction,
Li et al. (2026b) distinguishinternaluncertainty
(inherent to the model) fromexternaluncertainty
(arising from ambiguous or underspecified inputs).
Our work specifically investigates how agents han-
dle external uncertainty. Rather than considering
a formalized measure of confidence (e.g., a prob-
ability), our work evaluates uncertainty detection
behaviorally. That is, we assess whether agents
can reliably identify missing context and resolve it
through asking the user for clarification.
3 Method
3.1 Dataset and Evaluation Framework
We closely follow and adapt the interactive eval-
uation setting introduced by Vijayvargiya et al.
(2026b). We use their dataset (CMU LTI, 2024),
which is an underspecified variant of SWE-bench
Verified (Chowdhury et al., 2024), a human-
annotated subset of 500 GitHub issues derived from
the original SWE-bench dataset (Jimenez et al.,
2024). To construct their dataset, the authors uti-
lized GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) to summarize is-
sues into underspecified variants, removing impor-
tant details while preserving repository terminol-
ogy. Given the automatic nature of the construction
process, we performed an additional spot-check
analysis to verify dataset validity (Appendix A);
concrete examples are provided in Appendix D.
3.2 Agent Design
Agent FrameworkWe use the OpenHands
(Wang et al., 2025) agent framework for all ex-
periments. This framework enables an LLM to
iteratively write, execute, and debug code within
a secure sandbox environment, using tools for
navigating codebases, editing files and executing
Bash/Python scripts. To ensure comparability with
Vijayvargiya et al. (2026b), all agent setups are
provided with a maximum of 100 iterations.
Agent BackbonesWe evaluate two frontier back-
bone LLMs: Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025),
a proprietary model, and Kimi K2.6 (Moonshot
AI, 2026), an open-weight model. Both perform
competitively in many coding benchmarks, serving
as strong proxies for current agentic capabilities in
interactive software engineering settings.

3.3 Task Design
3.3.1 Baselines
Following Vijayvargiya et al. (2026b), we evaluate
our approach on the SWE-bench Verified dataset
against three distinct baseline configurations.
FULLThis is the standard SWE-bench setting,
where the agent is provided with a fully specified
version of the GitHub issue. The agent is prohibited
from interacting with a user, representing default
fully autonomous agent behavior.
HIDDENIn this configuration, the agent is pro-
vided with an underspecified version of the GitHub
issue where details are missing. As in the FULL
baseline, the agent cannot interact with a user.
INTERACTIVEBASELINEThe agent receives
an underspecified version of the GitHub issue (as

with the HIDDENbaseline) but can interact with
a simulated user who possesses the fully specified
issue. Importantly, the task prompt is modified to
explicitly inform the agent that the issue description
is incomplete, making it compulsory to query the
user before proceeding with any execution. Con-
sequently, this hardcoded instruction forces a pre-
determined conversational turn with the user rather
than evaluating independent information-seeking
behavior.
3.3.2 Uncertainty-Aware Agents
To investigate how agents can independently detect
and resolve underspecification, we propose two
scaffolds. We refer to these as “uncertainty-aware”
since they are designed to adapt their information-
seeking behavior based on the extent of missing
context. While these agents can query the same
simulated user as the INTERACTIVEBASELINE,
they do not rely on a hardcoded interaction prompt,
instead using the default SWE-bench task prompt.
Because they receive no prior warning that the issue
is underspecified, they must independently identify
missing context and query the simulated user only
when they determine it is necessary.
UNCERTAINTY-AWARE(SINGLE)In this con-
figuration, a single coding agent is prompted at
each turn to check for underspecification and, if
detected, to query the user. We refer to the agent
hereafter as UA-SINGLE.
UNCERTAINTY-AWARE(MULTI)We investi-
gate whether leveraging a multi-agent scaffold can
improve underspecification detection. By assign-
ing specialized roles to multiple LLMs, multi-agent
systems can tackle increasingly complex tasks
(Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Park et al.,
2023). As illustrated in Figure 1, to decouple code
execution from underspecification detection, we
design a multi-agent scaffold consisting of two
agents. TheMain Agentis equipped with stan-
dard tools to navigate repositories, edit files, and
execute code. TheIntent Agentmonitors the state
history at each turn to determine if the user’s intent
or repository context contains missing information.
The Intent Agent is provided with its own dedi-
cated system prompt (detailed in Appendix B.2)
and has access to a single tool that outputs a binary
decision indicating whether clarification is required.
Whenever the Intent Agent detects underspecifica-
tion, the Main Agent’s next action is constrained
to query the user. In our experiments, both agents

5 Conclusion
We investigated the abilities of LLM agents to act
as collaborators by independently identifying miss-
ing information and seeking clarification in under-
specified software engineering tasks. We intro-
duced an uncertainty-aware multi-agent scaffold
that isolates the role of underspecification detection.
Evaluated on an underspecified variant of SWE-
bench Verified, this system achieved a 69.40% re-
solve rate across both proprietary and open-weight
LLM backbones that effectively closed the perfor-
mance gap with an autonomous agent operating on
a fully specified issue.
Our results show that the multi-agent scaffold
exhibits well-calibrated information-seeking behav-
ior, accurately recognizing when an issue was al-
ready resolvable and refraining from unnecessary
interaction on simpler tasks. Our results also reveal
that underlying model capability shapes clarifica-
tion behavior: while both LLM backbones benefit
from the multi-agent scaffold, Kimi K2.6 exhibits
higher query rates and poorer calibration, suggest-
ing that robust uncertainty-aware interaction may
require targeted training beyond architectural de-
sign alone. These findings present a promising step
towards deploying agents not only as autonomous
coding assistants, but as proactive collaborators
capable of detecting and resolving external uncer-
tainty arising from real-world underspecification.
Limitations
User SimulatorOur evaluation relies on an
LLM-based user simulator to provide withheld in-
formation. While we implemented strict guardrails
to prevent unintended leakage and generally ob-
served reasonable simulator responses, recent stud-
ies highlight that LLM-simulated users can be un-

reliable proxies for human behavior, often being
unnaturally cooperative and failing to reflect the
nuance and variance of real human users (Naous
et al., 2026; Seshadri et al., 2026). Results may
therefore vary if real human users interact with the
agents that we presented in this work.
Prompting and TrainingWhile our most suc-
cessful approach relies on a multi-agent scaffold
with tailored prompts for each agent, it demon-
strates that frontier models possess the latent ca-
pacity to monitor their own uncertainty and proac-
tively seek clarification out of the box. Rather
than relying on prompting specialized agents, fu-
ture work could explore utilizing these success-
ful interaction trajectories to train single models
with standard finetuning or reinforcement learning
(RL) techniques to natively exhibit this calibrated,
information-seeking behavior (e.g., Andukuri et al.,
2024; Bhargava et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026).
Model Generalization and CostOur experi-
ments were conducted using both a proprietary
model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) and an open-weight
model (Kimi K2.6), demonstrating that our multi-
agent scaffold generalizes across model families.
By relying on frontier models, our evaluation in-
curred a non-trivial financial cost (detailed in Ap-
pendix E). Furthermore, previous studies indicate
that smaller open-weight models severely lack the
calibration required for interactive underspecifica-
tion (Vijayvargiya et al., 2026b), even with explicit
prompting. The current findings may therefore be
limited to frontier models and may not directly
translate to smaller open-source models.
Ethical Considerations
Our work investigated the ability of LLM agents to
detect external uncertainty and seek clarification on
underspecified software engineering tasks. While
our results demonstrate that uncertainty-aware scaf-
folds can effectively resolve underspecified GitHub
issues, SWE-bench Verified represents only a sub-
set of software engineering tasks. As such, our
empirical findings should not be extrapolated to
suggest that agents will reliably detect missing in-
formation in other, often high-stakes environments,
such as security-critical applications without fur-
ther experimentation.
We also acknowledge the environmental and fi-
nancial costs associated with the deployment of
multi-agent systems. As detailed in our cost break-
down (Appendix E), the increased inference over-
head in multi-agent systems is a trade-off that must
be weighed against the gains in developer produc-
tivity.
AI Use.We used an AI assistant to assist with
experimental code generation, and help improve
the clarity and flow of the writing while revising
the paper. However, all original writing, research
conceptualization, methods, experiments, and anal-
yses were performed by the authors, and any AI
outputs were carefully verified.
