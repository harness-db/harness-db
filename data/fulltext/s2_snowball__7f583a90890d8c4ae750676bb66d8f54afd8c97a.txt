ABSTRACT

Traditional portfolio management systems often rely on static rules or fixed prompts, which
limits their ability to adapt to changing market conditions. This paper proposes a closed -loop
multi-agent decision framework that introduces prompt -level learning as a scalable alternative
to full model retraining. The architecture comprises specialised agents for market signal
extraction, sentiment analysis, macroeconomic interpretation, risk control, and portfolio
construction, all coordinated through the DSPy framework and powered by Llama 3.1 8B.A key
contribution is a feedback -driven optimisation mechanism that refines agent prompts using
realised trading outcomes without human intervention. Moderate drawdowns trigger
incremental prompt updates, while severe drawdowns activate full prompt reconfiguration.
Empirical evaluation on a six -year dataset (2015 –2020) shows that the system achieves
cumulative returns above 80% with improved risk -adjusted performance (Sharpe > 1.5),
outperforming a SPY buy -and-hold benchmark, including during the COVID -19 market
disruption. Overall, the results highlight the potential of prompt -level adaptation for building
robust and autonomous financial decision systems.

1. INTRODUCTION

Traditional rule-based and early machine learning methods for managing investment portfolios
use fixed settings informed by historical data. Accordingly, they need regular manual updates to
change their behaviours and, as a result, do not adapt quickly to sudden changes in the market,
public sentiment, and geopolitical issues [1]. Therefore, more flexible and intelligent portfolio
management approaches are required to respond effectively to dynamic market conditions and
improve decision-making in increasingly complex financial environments.

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT‑4, Llama, and Mistral has
opened new possibilities for financial decision‑making by enabling reasoning across diverse data
sources, including price signals, company fundamentals, news, and social media sentiment [2].
Alongside this, multi‑agent systems have demonstrated that distributing analytical tasks across
specialised components can lead to more robust outcomes than single‑model approaches, hence,
closely reflecting how professional investment t eams operate across research, analysis, risk
management, and execution [3]. Recent frameworks such as TradingAgents, FINCON, and
FLAG‑TRADER combine these ideas and report strong risk‑adjusted performance [4][5][6].

However, they share a key limitation: agent behaviour remains static after deployment and cannot
adapt based on real trading outcomes. As a result, system performance degrades as market
conditions evolve. Moreover, no existing framework incorporates macroeconomic analysis agents
or dynamic trailing stop -loss mechanisms, leaving critical gaps in analytical coverage and risk
management.

This research addresses this limitation by proposing a closed‑loop multi‑agent portfolio
management system that enables continuous adaptation through dynamic prompt optimisation.
The system leverages specialised agents responsible for market signal extraction, sentiment
interpretation, macroeconomic analysis, risk control, and portfolio construction, coordinated to
generate daily trading decisions. Unlike existing approaches, it incorporates a
performance‑driven learning mechanism in which agent prompts are refined based on real trading
outcomes, which enables self‑improvement without manual intervention or model retraining. By
incorporating feedback directly in the decision -making process, the stability and risk awareness
of automated trading strategies improves, across changing market conditions. Therefore, the
system demonstrates a scalable and interpretable approach to building adaptive, autonomous
financial decision systems.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW

Increasing complexity of financial markets has attracted significant research interest in intelligent
portfolio management systems. For many years, traditional algorithmic and statistical approaches
dominated this field. However, recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and multi -
agent architectures have introduced new possibilities for developing autonomous and adaptive
trading systems.

This literature review examines five key areas that directly inform the proposed framework:
multi-agent LLM systems in financial trading, prompt optimisation frameworks, reinforcement
learning for portfolio management, financial sentiment analysis, and LLM -based agent
architectures. Therefore, the review highlights recent progress, identify key limitations in current
approaches, and motivate the need for a more adaptive, performance-driven system.

2.1 MULTI-AGENT LLM SYSTEMS IN FINANCIAL TRADING

Large Language Models have been rapidly adopted in financial trading, and the field has evolved
quickly from single -agent architectures towards collaborative multi -agent frameworks. Early
single-agent approaches have shown initial promise, with layered memory architectures
achieving a 61.78% return and a Sharpe ratio of 2.68 on TSLA by mimicking how human traders
restore information across different time horizons [4]. However, these systems struggled to
process the diverse range of financial information required for robust trading decisions,
particularly when handling longer financial texts and multi -source data simultaneously,
motivating the shift toward specialised multi-agent designs [4].

Distributing analytical roles across dedicated agents has overcome this limitation. TradingAgents
used a team-based structure with four analytical agents and a three -tier Risk Management Team,
achieving returns of 26.62% on AAPL and 24.36% on GOOGL with Sharpe ratios between 5.60
and 8.21. However, these came at the cost of over 20 tool calls per decision, and strong results
were only possible during calm market periods [5]. FINCON progressed this through a Manager -
Analyst hierarchy with Conceptual Verbal Reinforcement, achieving a 113.84% return with a
Sharpe ratio of 3.27. However, its hallucination rates increased when the number of managed
securities grew, and its drawdowns reached 45.22% on volatile stocks [6].

A critical limitation remains across all reviewed systems. Every framework relies on fixed
prompts that do not adapt based on actual trading outcomes, none incorporates macroeconomic

periods [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. These gaps inspire the self -correcting, macroeconomic-aware

2.2 PROMPT OPTIMISATION AND DSPY FRAMEWORKS

LLM performance is strongly influenced by prompting. In practice, prompt engineering often
relies on manual trial and error, which does not scale well across multi -stage pipelines, where a
small change in one prompt can break the entire system [10], [11]. Researchers recognised this
limitation and developed automatic optimisation methods, with DSPy treating LLM pipelines as
compliable text transformation graphs and improving accuracy from 33% to 82% on GSM8K for
GPT-3.5 within minutes [11]. Building on this, MIPROv2 uses a Bayesian surrogate model to
optimise instruction and few -shot demonstration, outperforming baselines on five of seven
diverse programmes by up to 13% in accuracy. Additionally, DSPy Assertions used runtime
Boolean constraints to further improve formatted output generation from 37.6% to 98.8% through
[12], [13], [14].

Other methods have shown that prompt optimisation is not limited to single -task environments.
ProTeGi applies textual gradient descent to continuously refine prompts with average
improvements of 4 –8%, while GEPA uses genetic evolution with a Pareto front to outperform
reinforcement learning methods by up to 20% using 35 times fewer rollouts, with notable
application to financial calculations using the CAPM formula [15], [16], [17]. These findings
confirm that the use of automatic prompt optimisation is both effective and applicable to financial
contexts, validating its use as the core adaptation mechanism in this research.

The existing gaps in prompt optimisation methods reviewed are that they focus on optimising
prompts for single tasks, including question answering, classification, or mathematical problems.
They do not apply these techniques systematically across multiple collaborating agents in a
trading system [11], [12], [14], [16]. Furthermore, they rely on static benchmarks rather than
actual trading performance. This study addresses both gaps by applying DSPy -based prompt
optimisation across seven collaborating agents driven by live portfolio performance.

2.3 Reinforcement Learning for Portfolio Management

Reinforcement learning has long been regarded as a promising approach for portfolio
management. It enables trading strategies to be learned through direct market interaction without
relying on static assumptions about return distributions [21], [22], [23]. The FinRL library
showed this potential through a standardised three -layer design that supports multiple RL
algorithms. It achieves annualised returns of 32 –45% on individual stocks and a 52.61%
ensemble return with a Sharpe ratio of 2.81 [23], [24]. The MTS framework expanded this with
time-aware embeddings and short -selling capabilities, achieving a 52.03% return with a Sharpe
ratio of 2.69 over a five -year period. FLAG -TRADER combined a compact 135 -million-
parameter large language model (LLM) with Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO), producing a
50.39% return on TSLA. Despite its much smaller size, it significantly outperformed GPT -4o on
the same task [22], [25], [30].

Reinforcement learning shows positive results, but its real-world application in trading is limited.
The systems are mostly evaluated over short periods or during favourable market conditions.

Therefore, it is difficult to determine how well they would perform during periods of market
stress. Further, they experience significant drawdowns during market crashes and rely on fixed
reward functions that do not adjust as market conditions and trading objectives change [25],

FLAG-TRADER, one of the most advanced systems reviewed, addresses some of these

or feedback mechanisms that continuously adjust agent behaviour based on trading outcomes and
market intelligence. Similarly, they do not employ the type of multi -agent collaboration that has
emerged in recent LLM -based trading research. These gaps present an opportunity to combine
the reasoning capabilities of LLMs with performance -driven prompt optimisation, adaptive
learning, and robust risk management. This combination forms the basis of the framework
proposed in this research.

2.4 FINANCIAL SENTIMENT ANALYSIS

Sentiment analysis of financial news and social media can provide valuable real -time trading
signals that can help investment managers detect changes in market mood before they are
reflected in asset prices. Early approaches, such as the Loughran –McDonald dictionary -based
method, analyse text by matching words against a predefined financial lexicon. However, these
methods pay little attention to word order, context, and the relationships between words. As a
result, their ability to predict market returns is limited [32].

The introduction of deep learning improved the accuracy of financial sentiment analysis
significantly. For example, FinBERT achieved 86% accuracy, improving the previous state of the
art by 15%. Instruct -FinGPT, a further example, increased accuracy to 88% on financial Twitter
data by applying instruction tuning to the LLaMA -7B model [33], [34]. Beyond improved
prediction accuracy, these models have also demonstrated practical value for trading. An OPT -
based model fine -tuned on Refinitiv news data generated cumulative returns of 355% between
2021 and 2023, with a Sharpe ratio of 3.05, providing strong evidence that LLM -derived
sentiment contains meaningful and exploitable trading signals [31].

However, analysing sentiment from social media remains challenging. Financial language on
platforms such as WallStreetBets often includes slang, humour, and sarcasm, which models, such
as FinBERT, can miss. To overcome this limitation, AIAP incorporated detailed annotator
guidelines directly into model prompts, which improved the sentiment classification accuracy by
5.9% [37].

Data quality, credibility and information presentation play a critical role in the performance of
financial sentiment analysis systems. Research using the FNSPID dataset demonstrated that the
credibility of the source significantly affects model accuracy. The study further showed that
lengthy news articles often distract models with irrelevant details, whereas concise summaries of
no more than three sentences lead to more stable and reliable results [38].

Despite recent advances, none of the reviewed systems combines sentiment analysis with
technical, fundamental, and macroeconomic analysis within a unified multi -agent framework. In
addition, current approaches do not use continuous feedback mechanisms that refine sentiment -

2.5 LLM AGENT FRAMEWORKS

Using large language models (LLMs) for complex decisions introduces a number of practical
challenges. When these systems are used in more demanding settings—especially where multiple
models interact —issues such as inconsistent outputs, unnecessary chains of reasoning, and
cascading hallucinations can emerge. Over time, these problems reduce the reliability of both the
information produced and the decisions based on it. To mitigate this, researchers have developed
structured agent frameworks that define how LLMs should reason, use external tools, and
collaborate more reliably.

Early research mainly focused on strengthening individual agents. A significant development was
Chain-of-Thought prompting, which improved step-by-step reasoning; increased performance on
the GSM8K mathematics benchmark from 18% to 57% [41]. Building on this, ReAct combined
reasoning with the use of external tools, leading to a 34% improvement on ALFWorld decision -
making tasks [42]. Reflexion went a step further by allowing agents to learn from their mistakes
in natural language. Through this form of self -reflection and memory, it achieved 91% accuracy
on the HumanEvalcoding benchmark, outperforming GPT-4’s reported 80.1% [45].

More recently, attention has shifted towards multi -agent systems, where different agents take on
specialised roles. This approach can be more effective for complex tasks. For instance, MetaGPT
showed that when agents follow Standardised Operating Procedures and communicate through a
shared message space, they can achieve 100% task completion on software engineering
benchmarks. It also found that clearly defined role structures consistently outperform more
loosely organised interactions [40], [46]. Similarly, Generative Agents demonstrated that
surprisingly rich and adaptive behaviour can emerge from relatively simple designs when
memory and reflection are included. In one example, 25 autonomous agents naturally formed
relationships and coordinated daily activities without any explicit programming of those
behaviours [47].

Despite these advances, existing agent frameworks are not designed specifically for financial
markets, where decisions must be made under significant time pressure and carry direct monetary
consequences. Current frameworks do not incorporate continuous prompt optimisation based on
real trading outcomes across multiple collaborating agents. Nor do they combine the reasoning
and tool-use capabilities of ReAct, the self -improvement mechanisms of Reflexion, and the role
specialisation demonstrated by MetaGPT within a unified financial decision-making system [40]-
[42], [45], [47], [48]. The framework proposed in this research addresses these gaps by
integrating these capabilities into a single architecture designed for autonomous portfolio
management.

2.6 FINANCIAL LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS

Applying large language models (LLMs) to stock market trading comes with a distinct set of
challenges. The financial data is highly time -sensitive, often becoming outdated within hours,
which makes real -world deployment difficult. In addition, developing proprietary LLMs can be
extremely expensive—for example, BloombergGPT required an estimated training cost of around
$3 million, which means such approach out of the reach of most investors and researchers [49].

To address this, FinGPT introduced a data -centric, five -layer framework that relies on
lightweight LoRA fine-tuning. This reduced adaptation costs dramatically to around $300, while
still achieving 82.1% sentiment accuracy using reinforcement learning on stock price signals. It
also outperformed both zero -shot ChatGPT and BloombergGPT in its evaluation setup [49].
Building on this, PIXIU provided a broader evaluation ecosystem for financial LLMs. It achieved
state-of-the-art performance on sentiment and news classification tasks. Still, its quantitative
reasoning showed limitations, which is largely due to limited mathematical pretraining [50].

In addition to assessing sentiment for financial decisions, various studies have examined how
LLMs can advance our understanding of the factors driving market movements. For instance,
LLMFactor used Sequential Knowledge-Guided Prompting to extract interpretable price -moving
factors from financial news. It achieved 63% accuracy on US markets and improved baseline
performance by up to 11% [51]. Similarly, the SEP framework combined summarisation,
explanation, and prediction with PPO -based self -reflection. This enabled stronger directional
forecasting from mixed sentiment signals [52], [53].

Collectively, these findings suggest that carefully designed, task -specific LLM systems with
lightweight adaptation can outperform large proprietary models, often at a fraction of the cost.
Despite these advances, most existing models still operate in isolation rather than as part of a
coordinated multi -agent system. They also do not incorporate continuous prompt optimisation
driven by actual trading performance over time [49] –[52]. The approach proposed in this work
directly addresses this gap by combining multiple specialised agents with DSPy -based dynamic
prompt optimisation, guided by real portfolio outcomes rather than static benchmark evaluations.

3. METHODOLOGY

This section describes the design, architecture, and evaluation of the proposed portfolio
management system. It first outlines the overall framework and its multi‑agent structure,
followed by a detailed description of the system architecture, specialised agents, and data
sources. The section then presents the dynamic prompt optimisation strategy and feedback
mechanism that enable continuous system adaptation. Finally, the experimental setup and
evaluation metrics are defined to assess system performance under different market conditions.

3.1 OVERVIEW OF THE PROPOSED FRAMEWORK

This research proposes a fully autonomous multi -agent portfolio management framework called
the Adaptive Closed -loop Multi -Agent Portfolio system (ACMAP). The system integrates
specialised analytical agents with dynamic prompt optimisation and a self -correcting feedback
loop.

As shown in Figure 1, the framework follows a structured pipeline. Financial data is processed by
specialised agents, including a Technical Analyst (extracting price -based signals using indicators
and historical price patterns), a Fundamental Analyst (assessing company financial health using
earnings, profitability, and balance sheet metrics), a Sentiment Analyst (interpreting market
sentiment from news and social media), and a Macro Analyst (analysing macroeconomic
conditions such as interest rates, inflation, and volatility to capture market-wide trends).

The Stock Picker analyses information it receives from all the analyst agents to determine trade
direction and conviction levels for each asset. Next, the Risk Manager receives and analyses the
output of the Stock Picker to apply position -level controls, which include stop -loss (automatic
sell threshold to cap losses), take -profit (automatic sell threshold to lock in gains), and exposure

Computer Science & Information Technology (CS & IT) 31

limits (maximum capital allocation to a single asset or sector). Finally, an Orchestrator integrates
all agent outputs with the current portfolio context to produce the final trading decisions.
The proposed system operates as a closed -loop architecture. It continuously monitors trading
performance, and it uses insights from the monitoring and from other analytical agents to refine
prompts within the DSPy framework. This design allows the proposed ACMAP architecture to
adapt to changing market conditions without requiring retraining or manual intervention. Thus, it
can adjust its behaviour as markets evolve, rather than relying on fixed rules. Moreover, this
multi-agent collaboration and performance -driven learning is intended to improve robustness
across different market regimes.

3.2 SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

The framework features a three -layer hierarchical multi -agent architecture: a data layer, an
analysis layer, and a decision layer. The data layer collects and organises raw financial
information each trading day, covering market price and volume data, company financial
statements, news articles, social media content from Reddit and X, and broader macroeconomic
indicators, with each data source carefully assigned to the agent best suited to interpret it.

The analysis layer contains the four specialist agents, namely Technical, Fundamental, Sentiment,
and Macro Analysts, each producing a directional signal of bullish, bearish, or neutral alongside a
confidence score. The outputs of the four analysts are then aggregated into a structured market
summary string and passed to the decision layer.

The decision layer consists of the Stock Picker, Risk Manager, and Orchestrator agents. The
Stock Picker and Risk Manager each receive the consolidated analytical signals, along with
current portfolio state and a plain -English performance summary. After analysing those details,
they produce trade recommendations and risk parameters that are included in the information sent
to the Orchestrator agent.

Then, the Orchestrator creates a comprehensive portfolio summary and issues final trade
instructions for each security. The trade instructions are passed through the confidence gate that
automatically prevents entry of any new positions with a confidence score below 0.6 and issues a
hold instruction instead. However, exit instructions will always be executed, regardless of the
confidence level.

Note that all agents execute sequentially rather than in parallel. This reflects Groq API rate limits,
and this approach ensures that each agent operates on the full information generated by preceding
agents before the next stage begins. Consequently, the Orchestrator makes its decisions based on
the consolidated outputs of all six preceding agents, therefore preserving collaborative decision -
making despite the sequential execution process.

This layered architecture and the confidence-gating mechanism ensure that every trading decision
considers the entire portfolio and the all the intelligence gathered by the agents. This is conceived
to minimise the risk of isolated decisions that may seem reasonable for individual assets but
could hurt overall portfolio performance.

The Technical Analyst agent looks at historical price and volume data to identify potential trading
opportunities. It uses a set of commonly used indicators, including the 14 -day Relative Strength
Index (RSI), Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD) and its signal line, 50 -day and
200-day Simple Moving Averages (SMAs), the 14 -day Average True Range (ATR), and textual

The Fundamental Analyst focuses on the underlying financial health of a company. It analyses
metrics such as, company revenue, net income, earnings per share, return on equity, debt -to-
equity ratio, and profit margin. In combination with the Technical Analyst, it helps the system
look at both business performance and market behaviour rather than relying on price data alone.
To add a wider perspective, the Macro Analyst considers broader economic conditions. It uses
indicators such as the VIX volatility index, Federal Reserve interest rates, the Consumer Price
Index (CPI), and year -on-year inflation. These help highlight the wider economic trends that
influence the portfolio's performance over time. This macro layer is a key part of the framework,
since many similar multi-agent systems tend to focus only on company and market-level signals.
Market behaviour is also shaped by investor sentiment, which can move prices before it shows up
in financial data or technical indicators. To capture this, the Sentiment Analyst reviews financial
news, analyst reports, and social media posts from Reddit and X. Using the language
understanding abilities of a Large Language Model (LLM), it produces a near-real-time sentiment
signal that can sometimes pick up early shifts in market mood.

Together, these four agents give different but complementary views of the market. Each produces
a directional signal—bullish, bearish, or neutral—along with a confidence score between 0 and 1.
These form the input to the next stage of the system.

The Stock Picker then brings these signals together into a single recommendation for each stock.
Alongside the LLM’s own judgement, it calculates a conviction score based on how much the
agents agree. Strong conviction is assigned when at least three analysts agree, or when two agree
with an average confidence above 0.70. Moderate conviction is assigned when two agree or when
the average confidence is above 0.60.

These outputs are then passed to the Risk Manager, which reviews the risk profile of each trade
and sets stop-loss and take-profit levels using ATR and VIX. Position sizing is adjusted based on
the conviction level from the Stock Picker, so higher-confidence trades receive larger allocations,
while lower-confidence ones are scaled down.

Finally, the Orchestrator pulls everything together. It combines the outputs of all agents with
current portfolio holdings, recent trading history, and performance context. Working at the
portfolio level, it produces final trading decisions across all tickers at once, making sure that each
decision is considered in relation to the whole portfolio rather than in isolation.

3.4 Dynamic Prompt Optimisation

Rather than relying on fixed, manually created prompts, the proposed framework uses the DSPy
framework to automatically fine-tune the instructions. It guides each agent based on real portfolio
performance. After every 10 -day simulation chunk, all six optimisable agents receive a plain -
English reward context string summarising recent performance across metrics such as drawdown,
turnover, and signal accuracy, allowing them to adjust their behaviour immediately without any
formal optimisation step. The 10 -day window balances responsiveness and stability: a shorter
window of a day or two would cause agents to overreact to normal market fluctuations, and a
longer window of 30 or 60 days would delay detection of genuine performance degradation
before significant losses accumulate.

When performance degrades further and drawdown crosses 2%, the framework initiates a formal
prompt optimisation step. If drawdown falls between 2% and 8%, a Negative BootstrapFewShot
procedure relabels the current chunk's examples as hold. It teaches agents to be more cautious in
similar market conditions. If drawdown exceeds 8%, Emergency MIPROv2 is triggered, fully
rewriting the prompts of all six optimisable agents using a light configuration that balances
optimisation quality against computational cost.

The six agents whose prompts are optimised are the Technical Analyst, Fundamental Analyst,
Sentiment Analyst, Macro Analyst, Stock Picker, and Risk Manager. The Orchestrator is
deliberately excluded from formal prompt optimisation, operating on its original prompt
throughout and relying solely on its daily plain -English performance context to adapt its
portfolio-wide decisions. This design choice ensures that the portfolio -level decision maker
maintains consistent reasoning behaviour while the analytical and signal -generation agents adapt
to changing market conditions below it.

3.5 Feedback Loop and Self-Correction Mechanism

The self -correction mechanism operates at two separate levels (daily and 10 -day period) that
work together to ensure the system continuously improves without any manual intervention.
First, on a daily basis, the simulator evaluates portfolio performance to gain insights and enable
early intervention. It produces a plain -English context string summarising key metrics (including
daily return, cumulative return, drawdown, number of trades executed, and signal accuracy),
which is passed directly to the Stock Pi cker, Risk Manager, and Orchestrator to use for the next
day. This relatively quick insight allows agents to adjust their behaviour in response to recent

34 Computer Science & Information Technology (CS & IT)

outcomes. For example, it may lower trading frequency when excessive turnover is noticed or
become more cautious when the drawdown increases.

Second, every 10 -day simulation, the system evaluates the overall reward function (including
accuracy, returns, Sharpe ratio, drawdown, and turnover). These results create a detailed
performance feedback string for all six optimisable agents before the next chunk starts. If the
evaluation shows that drawdown has exceeded one of the two predefined thresholds, the feedback
loop moves from a simple context update to a formal prompt optimisation step, as outlined in
Section 3.6.

Together, these two feedback levels create a continuously self -correcting system that responds to
performance at both the daily and broader window levels. This combination of the immediacy of
daily performance signals with the deeper structural correction provided by periodic prompt
optimisation ensures that agents are updated with the relevant and reality-based guidance.

3.6 Portfolio Management and Risk Controls

The portfolio management layer translates the Orchestrator's high -level trade instructions into
executed positions while enforcing a set of risk controls governing how positions are sized,
monitored, and closed. The framework supports both long and short positions, distinguishing it
from the majority of existing automated portfolio management systems that are limited to buy -
and-hold strategies.

Position sizing is based on the level of conviction from the Stock Picker. Strong conviction
permits a maximum position size of 15% of the total portfolio value; moderate conviction allows
for the standard maximum of 10%; weak conviction restricts the position to 5%. All positions
require a minimum holding period of 20 days to avoid excessive turnover from daily signal
changes.

Every open position is protected by a dynamic trailing stop -loss and take -profit mechanism that
runs throughout the simulation. When a trade is opened, the Risk Manager agent determines the
initial stop -loss and take -profit levels using the individual stock's Average True Range (ATR)
and the current VIX level; hence, the thresholds reflect prevailing market conditions. These levels
are then reviewed at the end of each trading day. Note that, for long positions, the stop -loss
moves upwards as the price rises, while for short positions, it moves downwards as the price falls.
Therefore, the stop is only adjusted in a useful direction that allows gains to be protected while
limiting potential losses.

Before any new trade is opened, the check_exit_levels() function checks and forces -closes any
position that has crossed its stop-loss or take-profit threshold. It is a safety net independent of the
Orchestrator's daily decisions. This two -layer exit mechanism, combining the Orchestrator's
informed decision-making with the automatic force -close function, ensures that no position can
remain open beyond its risk boundaries regardless of what the Orchestrator decides on any given
day. Short positions follow the same sizing and exit logic in reverse.

3.8 Data Sources and Collection

The dataset covers six years of financial market activity from January 2015 to December 2020
across 13 securities comprising 11 individual equities and two broad -market ETFs, the SPDR
S&P 500 ETF and the Invesco QQQ Trust. Six distinct data streams are merged at a daily
frequency and joined on a shared date and ticker key: daily price and volume data are
downloaded from Yahoo Finance with automatic adjustment for splits and dividends. Seven
technical indicators are computed using pandas -ta. Three macroeconomic indicators, namely
VIX, Federal Funds Rate, and CPI, alongside a year -on-year change series, are fetched from the
Federal Reserve Economic Data service and forward-filled to avoid look-ahead bias, and sell-side
analyst ratings are sourced from Yahoo Finance and converted from free -text strings into
numerical scores between -1 and +1 through a three-pass mapping process.

Financial news sentiment is streamed from a Hugging Face corpus of over 26 million records
filtered to the study window and relevant securities, while social media sentiment combines
approximately 3.72 million tweets and 250,000 Reddit WallStreetBets posts sourced from
Kaggle. All text is cleaned, truncated to 300 characters, and scored using Mistral Devstral 2 with
123 billion parameters via the NVIDIA inference API, with daily scores aggregated to a mean per
ticker and missing days filled with a neutral value of zero. It should be noted that the Twitter
dataset only covers up to December 2019, meaning no social media signal is available during the
COVID-19 crash period.

Ground truth labels are generated from the 30 -day forward return of each security's adjusted
closing price, computed separately per ticker to avoid look -ahead bias, with returns above 2%
labelled as buy, returns below -2% labelled as short, and anything within this range labelled as
hold. These thresholds were chosen to reflect economically meaningful price moves that exceed
typical transaction costs on a trading platform.

3.9 Experimental Setup

The framework is evaluated through backtesting across two distinct evaluation windows. The
dataset is divided chronologically with no random shuffling into Period 1 (Jan 2015 – Jun 2018),
an Excluded Period (Jul 2018 – Jun 2019), and Period 2 (Jul 2019 – Dec 2020); they respectively
have 11440, 3250 and 4940 instances. The Excluded Period is omitted for two reasons. First, it
helps keep the computational cost of the experiments manageable, as each trading day requires

Computer Science & Information Technology (CS & IT) 37

multiple LLM inference calls across six analytical agents and the Orchestrator. Second, the
remaining evaluation windows capture distinctly different market conditions.

The two evaluation windows capture very different market environments. Period 1 spans a
prolonged bull market and the 2018 correction; Period 2 includes the period leading up to
COVID-19, the sharp market downturn that followed, and the subsequent recovery. Assessing
the framework across these contrasting conditions provides a clearer picture of how it performs
as market dynamics change.

All agents are powered by Llama 3.1 8B served through the Groq API. This model was selected
largely because of its speed and relatively low inference cost, both of which are important when
running simulations over multiple years of trading data. Performance is compared against a
simple SPY buy-and-hold strategy, which serves as a widely recognised baseline that any active
portfolio management system should aim to outperform. To ensure reproducibility, a fixed
random seed of 42 is used throughout the experiments.

Although SPY buy-and-hold provides a transparent and widely recognised benchmark, it does not
capture the full range of approaches discussed in Section 2. In particular, the present study does
not include direct comparisons with recent LLM-based trading systems or traditional quantitative
strategies such as momentum and equal -weight portfolios. While such comparisons would
provide additional context for interpreting the results, the SPY benchmark offers a clear and
practical baseline against which the effectiveness of the proposed framework can be evaluated.
Exploring a broader set of benchmarks represents a valuable direction for future work.
The simulation is initialised with a starting portfolio value of $100,000. To better reflect real -
world trading conditions, a transaction cost of 0.1% is applied whenever a position is opened or
closed.

3.10 Evaluation Metrics

The performance of the proposed framework is evaluated using a composite reward function that
brings together five metrics. Each of metrics is a assigned a weighting in the composite reward
function according to its importance in portfolio management. The Sharpe ratio carries the
highest weight at 30%, reflecting its role as a standard measure of risk -adjusted performance;
Total returns follow at 25%, ensuring profitability remains central; a turnover penalty accounts
for 20%, discouraging excessive trading that increases costs and often reflects noise rather than
signal; Drawdown is weighted at 15% to capture peak-to-trough losses; 10% is assigned to signal
quality as a final performance check.

The combination of these metrics gives a comprehensive and realistic view of performance,
addressing profitability, risk, trading efficiency, and downside protection. Looking at just one
measure on its own can easily give a distorted picture. For example, a strategy might deliver
strong returns but with large drawdowns, or appear highly accurate simply because it trades too
frequently. By combining multiple signals into a single reward, the framework encourages more
balanced behaviour and offers a more realistic sense of how the system would perform in practice
as an autonomous portfolio manager.

4. RESULTS

This section shows the results of backtesting over two contrasting time periods. Period 1 includes
a long bull market followed by the 2018 market downturn. Period 2 covers the growth before
COVID-19, the market crash during the pandemic, and the quick recovery that followed. In both

cases, we started with an initial portfolio value of $100,000 and included a transaction cost of
0.1% for each trade. We compared performance to a SPY buy-and-hold strategy.

In Period 1, the framework achieved a total return of 84.76% with a Sharpe ratio of 1.52. In
Period 2, it provided a similar return of 85.40% and improved the Sharpe ratio to 1.79, even
during one of the most turbulent times in recent market history. A key finding of this study is the
framework producing consistent returns across these very different market conditions.

4.1 Period 1 (January 2015 to June 2018)

During Period 1, the portfolio grew from $100,000 to $184,761 over 800 trading days; therefore,
it produced a total return of 84.76%, which compares favourably against the SPY benchmark
return of 38% over the same period. It reached a peak of $191,233 on 12 March 2018 before
declining during the early 2018 market correction. Even with this pullback, the maximum
drawdown remained contained at 17.42%, while the annualised Sharpe ratio reached 1.52,
comfortably above the commonly accepted benchmark of 1.0.

Trading activity reflects the market conditions over the period. A total of 760 trades were
executed in Period 1. Notably, 499 out of the 760 were buy orders -- consistent with the broadly
bullish regime of Period 1. For closed positions, 130 out of 211 ended with profit, which is a win
rate of 61.6%. Furthermore, performance was particularly strong on long positions, which
achieved a 67.6% -win rate and an average gain of 70.75%, compared with an average loss of
15.58%. Therefore, profit -to-loss ratio was about 4.5:1, showing that the trailing stop -loss
successfully minimised losses and let profits increase.

Out of the 14,697 signals generated by Stock Picker, only 954 were executed by the Orchestrator;
only 6.5% of the signals were acted on. This low execution rate shows that the decision -making
process is very selective. Most potential trades are filtered out before they are executed.

Across 80 ten -day evaluation windows, 57 required no optimisation, 22 triggered Negative
BootstrapFewShot, and one triggered Emergency MIPROv2 when drawdown reached 8.13%.
Following this intervention, the composite reward score improved from 0.0226 to 0.0641 within
the same evaluation window. More broadly, Negative BootstrapFewShot improved performance
in 16 of the 21 measurable cases, representing a success rate of 76%. Over the course of the
period, the composite reward increased from 0.3192 in the first evaluation window to a peak of
0.5555 towards the end of the period.

4.3 Comparative Analysis

Given the difference in sample size between the two evaluation windows, the comparison should
be interpreted in a descriptive rather than strictly comparative sense. The primary contribution is ,
therefore, to assess whether the framework remains stable across distinct market regimes and
whether performance characteristics are broadly consistent rather than identical in magnitude..
Across both windows, the framework maintained broadly similar total returns (84.76% in Period
1 and 85.40% in Period 2), despite exposure to very different market conditions. Drawdown
levels were also closely aligned at approximately 17% in both cases, suggesting a stable level of
risk control across regimes. At the same time, differences in return efficiency are visible in the
Sharpe ratio, which increased from 1.52 to 1.79 in the second window.

The long -side win rate increased from 67.6% to 81.8%, while average losses decreased from
15.58% to 6.21%, resulting in a higher profit -to-loss ratio (4.5:1 to 6.3:1). These shifts indicate a
change in trade distribution and outcome quality rather than simply scale effects, though they
should be interpreted with caution given the differing number of trades across windows.

The findings of this study show that the ACMAP framework yields similar returns in two
different market environments. It generated 84.76% in Period 1, which is includes a bull market
period and the 2018 correction. Similarly, it returned 85.40% in Period 2, which covers the
COVID-19 crash and recovery. Furthermore, their drawdown levels were comparable. Although
the two windows differ in length and are therefore not directly comparable, the results suggest
that the framework remained effective across a range of market conditions rather than being
dependent on a single favourable regime.

One possible explanation lies in the complementary nature of the multi -agent architecture. The
Technical, Fundamental, Sentiment, and Macro analysts each provide a different perspective on
the market, allowing decisions to be informed by multiple sources of information rather than a
single signal type. In practice, these agents often produce conflicting assessments. The role of the
Stock Picker is therefore not simply to aggregate signals, but to balance competing viewpoints
before generating a recommendation. This design may help reduce reliance on any one source of
information and could contribute to the framework's ability to operate across changing market
environments.

The results also provide some support for the use of adaptive prompt optimisation. Optimisation
activity increased substantially during Period 2, which contained the most volatile market
conditions in the study. While the observed improvement rates do not demonstrate that
optimisation was consistently beneficial, they suggest that the feedback mechanism remained
active during periods of market stress and was capable of adjusting agent prompts when
performance deteriorated. This behaviour is consistent with the intended design of ACMAP as a
closed-loop system that adapts through prompt refinement rather than model retraining. Several
limitations should be kept in mind when interpreting these results. The study is based on
backtesting rather than live trading, and real -world deployment would introduce additional
factors such as slippage, market impact, and execution delays that are not captured here. In
addition, while SPY buy -and-hold provides a clear and widely used baseline, a broader set of
benchmarks (e.g., quantitative and LLM -based trading methodologies) for a more thorough
assessment. Future work should therefore consider comparisons against a wider range of
quantitative and LLM -based trading strategies to better position ACMAP relative to existing
approaches.

The deployment of AI -driven trading systems raises practical considerations around governance
and oversight. While the proposed framework is designed to enable transparency (decision paths
and risk controls are logged), the system is meant to be autonomous; therefore, real-world use
would still require human supervision to ensure accountability. These requirements are
particularly relevant in systems where multiple components contribute to a single trading
decision.For this reason, ACMAP should be viewed as a research prototype for decision support
and controlled automation rather than a production -ready trading system. The focus of this work
is on exploring whether a multi -agent, prompt -driven architecture can produce consistent
portfolio behaviour under varying market conditions, rather than on direct deployment in live
financial markets.

5. CONCLUSION

This research introduced a fully autonomous multi -agent portfolio management system that
combines Large Language Model reasoning with adaptive prompt optimisation and a closed-loop
feedback mechanism. In backtesting, the framework produced strong and relatively stable

performance across distinct market environments, including periods of heightened volatility, and
consistently outperformed a passive benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis.

Rather than relying on a single source of insight, the system draws on a mix of technical,
fundamental, sentiment, and macroeconomic signals. That combination seems to matter,
particularly when conditions shift, as it reduces dependence on any one view of the market.
Alongside this, the results suggest that prompt -level optimisation can play a useful role in
adaptation, with clearer improvements emerging during more volatile periods.

More generally, the study points to an alternative way of thinking about portfolio systems: not as
models that need constant retraining, but as structured agent systems that can adjust through
feedback and prompt refinement. This offers a practical and potentially more interpretable route
to building adaptive trading systems.

Future work will move towards live trading evaluation and explore how the approach behaves
with larger models, additional asset classes, and more complex market conditions.
