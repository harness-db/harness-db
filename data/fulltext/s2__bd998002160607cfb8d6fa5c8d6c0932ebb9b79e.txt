Abstract
Radiology, with its heterogeneous modalities,
anatomies, and evolving protocols, is a natu-
ral yet high-stakes testbed for agentic AI. As
LLMs grow more capable and tool ecosystems
such as MCP and Agent Skills more complex,
their capability boundaries in radiology remain
unclear. We introduceRadA-BenchPlat, a
two-layer benchmark spanning idealized rea-
soning and real-world execution: asynthetic
layerwith 2.2k clinician-verified records, 24.2k
QA pairs, and 10 tool categories under diverse
availability settings; and areal-environment
layerpairing 165 2D/3D cases with executable
tools for grounding, diagnosis, and report gen-
eration. We find that high completion rates
under idealized settings do not carry over to
real environments, where the best agents trail
their upper bound by∼15% and drop to 0.327
on long-chain tasks. Prompting strategies (few-
shot exemplars, multi-agent coordination) and
AutoTB (on-the-fly synthesis of missing tools)
mitigate execution drift and breakdowns in dif-
ficult scenarios—lifting execution success to
94.5%—yet remain not fully reliable. Over-
all, our findings suggest that radiology agents
are no longer limited to conceptual demonstra-
tions, but are beginning to show potential for
real-world applications.
1 Introduction
Recent efforts have introduced “radiology gener-
alist” models designed to handle diverse imaging
tasks within a unified framework (Wu et al., 2025;
Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023).
However, these monolithic systems still struggle
with radiological complexity across heterogeneous
imaging modalities, anatomical regions, patholo-
gies, and evolving diagnostic protocols. In parallel,
LLM-powered agent systems (Tang et al., 2024;

synthetic and real-environment benchmark layers.
Jin et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024) reframe complex
problem solving as tool-mediated planning and ex-
ecution, where an LLM interprets requests, selects
specialized tools, and synthesizes intermediate out-
puts. This paradigm is increasingly relevant as
agent ecosystems move beyond static tool lists to-
ward heterogeneous infrastructures, such as Model
Context Protocol (MCP) servers (Zhu et al., 2026)
and Agent Skills (Xu et al., 2026), where capabili-
ties, interfaces, and dependencies vary substantially
across environments. As modern LLMs become
more agentic, radiology offers a high-stakes setting
for examining whether they can reliably coordi-
nate complex tool chains; however, their capability
boundaries, robustness, and danger zones on radiol-
ogy tasks remain insufficiently and systematically
studied.
We therefore focus on two questions.First, if
individual tool quality is abstracted away, how well
can current LLMs understand radiological contexts,
translate clinical queries into executable workflows,
and invoke tools across multi-step tasks?Second,

in realistic radiology settings with executable tools,
what level of task completion and clinical utility
can current agent systems achieve?
To address these questions, we introduceRadA-
BenchPlat, a benchmark with two complementary
layers (Figure 1).First, the synthetic layer inte-
grates patient records, task-related QA pairs, and
simulated radiology tool sets: 2,200 records across
6 anatomical regions and 5 imaging modalities,
cross-validated by three clinicians on 550 sampled
cases; 11 task types yielding 24,200 QA pairs; and
10 tool categories organized into baseline, redun-
dant, insufficient, and differentiated settings.Sec-
ond, the real-environment layer uses 165 cases
from 2D and 3D datasets for three representative
tasks—organ/anomaly grounding, diagnosis with
grounding clues, and report generation—and in-
troduces executable radiology-model tools from
M3Builder (Feng et al., 2025), nnU-Net (Isensee
et al., 2021), and RP3D-DM (Zheng et al., 2024).
Our synthetic evaluation reveals a central insight:
current LLM agents can often outline plausible ra-
diology workflows, but their idealized upper bound
is limited by execution rather than reasoning alone.
Reliability degrades when tasks require long-chain
dependency tracking, precise I/O transfer, and
grounded final synthesis; in high-difficulty settings,
the best completion remains only 0.327. Neverthe-
less, few-shot workflow exemplars and multi-agent
coordination alleviate long-chain control failures,
while AutoTB, inspired by self-evolving agents that
create tools on demand (Cai et al., 2023; Wölflein
et al., 2025), addresses missing-capability cases by
requesting missing tools in the synthetic layer and
constructing them in the real-environment layer.
Together, these results show that workflow-level
scaffolding can raise the attainable ceiling.
Real-environment evaluation sharpens this find-
ing: even the best-performing agentic systems re-
main about 15% below the idealized setting, reveal-
ing a persistent gap between reasoning over tools
and executing with them. AutoTB narrows this gap
in regular and intermediate missing-tool scenarios
(94.5% and 86.8% execution success), yet 48% us-
age errors show that newly built tools still require
robust integration. Expert review suggests practical
value in routine and intermediate cases (4.2 and 3.6
above a 3-point threshold), while complex cases re-
main clinically fragile. These findings suggest that
radiology agents are ready for controlled clinical-
scenario testing, but their real-world effectiveness
must still be carefully validated.
2 Methods
RadA-BenchPlat is organized as two complemen-
tary layers. The synthetic layer builds an idealized
environment with perfect simulated tools, which
decouples failures of the agent core from failures
of tool execution and stress-tests a broad range of
scenarios that limited real data cannot cover. The
real-environment layer instead measures what cur-
rent agentic systems actually achieve on executable
radiology tasks and what limits them in practice.

Radiology Tool Sets.To simulate diverse clinical
tool environments, we define 10 high-level radi-
ology tool categories: Anatomy Classifier (AC),
Modality Classifier (MC), Organ Segmentor (OS),
Anomaly Detector (AD), Imaging Diagnoser (ID),
Grounded Diagnoser (GD), Biomarker Quantifier
(BQ), Indicator Calculator (IC), Report Generator
(RG), and Treatment Planner (TP). Each tool is rep-
resented by a “tool card” (Figure 2d) specifying its
category, properties, capabilities, required/optional
inputs, and performance sampled from clinically
plausible ranges, enabling diverse tool variations.
We further define four evaluation tool-set condi-
tions:“Baseline”provides a minimal solvable set
of 12 tools across 10 categories, with BQ and IC
each containing two tools for organ- and anomaly-
level assessment;“Redundant”adds irrelevant
tools at regular (12–15 tools, at most one extra per
category), medium (27–34 tools, 2–3 extras per
category), and high (fixed 169 tools) levels to test
filtering in noisy settings;“Insufficient”creates

unsolvable sets through config1 missing an entire
category (14–17 tools), config2 modality/anatomy
mismatches (15–17 tools), or config3 missing re-
quired capabilities (fixed 18 tools), with increasing
recognition difficulty; and“Differentiated”offers
overlapping tools (17–18 tools) with distinct per-
formance levels, requiring selection of the most
accurate tool. The full definition of each tool cate-
gory, together with its input/output schema and an
example tool card, is provided in Appendix A.4.
Real Radiology Environments.In addition to
synthetic QA pairs, we construct a test set of 165
real-world cases from Chest Imagenome (Wu et al.,
2021) and RadGenome-BrainMRI datasets (Lei
et al., 2026), covering three tasks: organ/anomaly
grounding, diagnosis, and report generation. Each
case preserves the original dataset components, in-
cluding the clinical question, image, and ground-
truth annotation or report. For executable eval-
uation, we build the real-case tool pool using
M3Builder (Feng et al., 2025), covering segmen-
tation, detection, and report-generation tools, and
further supplement it with nnU-Net (Isensee et al.,
2021) and RP3D-DM (Zheng et al., 2024) for seg-
mentation and diagnosis alternatives. We further in-
stantiate 660 insufficient-tool scenarios from cases
in these datasets, enabling evaluation of automated
tool building under real conditions. Case-selection
criteria and the provenance, modality, and pathol-
ogy coverage of the two source collections are de-
tailed in Appendix A.7, and the construction of the
executable tool pool is described in Appendix A.8.
2.2 Benchmarking LLMs as Agent Cores
Main Workflow Formulation.The LLM-based
agent system consists of three main compo-
nents (Figure 3a): an LLM agent core ( Φ), a set
of specialized tools (T={t 1, t2, . . . , tm}), and
a memory bank (B). Given a clinical query Q
and patient information, Φ first parses the query,
decomposes it into sub-tasks according to the avail-
able tools inT , and initializesB with relevant pa-
tient data. For each sub-task, the agent selects
and invokes the appropriate tool, stores intermedi-
ate outputs inB, and uses these outputs to guide
subsequent tool calls. Finally, Φ synthesizes the
accumulated evidence into a response that directly
addresses the clinical query.
Prompting and Automated Tool Building.To ad-
dress the limited performance of LLMs on complex
tasks, we introduce four prompting enhancement
strategies (Figure 3b).Prompt Back-propagation
iteratively refines prompts through three rounds
of automated feedback and manual adjustment
based on 220 QA pairs under “Redundant” and
“Insufficient” tool-set conditions, after which the
refined prompt is applied across the benchmark.
Self-reflectionasks the agent to verify input cor-
rectness before tool invocation, supported by an
automated verifier to reduce execution errors.Few-
shot Learningprovides exemplar workflows and
tool templates to standardize planning and tool use,
whileMulti-agent Collaborationrestructures the
agent into a planner, executor, and summarizer for
task decomposition, I/O management, and final
response synthesis. AutoTB follows different pro-
tocols in the two benchmark layers. In the syn-
thetic layer, the agent receives M 3Builder usage
instructions and is evaluated on whether it cor-
rectly triggers AutoTB and submits a build request
matching the condition-specific missing fields: cat-
egory for config1, anatomy and modality for con-
fig2, and category with the missing capability for
config3. No model is trained in this layer; after
a request passes verification, the requested tool is
simulated as available so that subsequent orchestra-
tion can be evaluated. In the real-environment layer,
M3Builder (Feng et al., 2025) is actually invoked
to train and validate the requested tool, register
successful tools in the executable pool, and record
construction and usage outcomes in its logs. Ap-
pendix A.9 gives the verification rules, executable
pseudo-code, and specific traces for both layers.
Real-Case Evaluation Protocol.For real-world
testing, we use the original clinical question, im-
age, and reference annotation/report from each
case without converting them into synthetic pa-
tient records. The agent receives the clinical in-
put and must complete the task by selecting from
executable radiology tools rather than relying on
idealized tool outputs. On the 165-case test set, we
evaluate organ/anomaly grounding, diagnosis with
grounding clues, and standard report generation
using task-specific automatic metrics, including
Dice/Jaccard for grounding, Accuracy/Jaccard for
diagnosis, and RadGraph-F1/RaTE for reports. We
also collect LLM-based ratings and blinded human
ratings from two expert radiologists to assess clini-
cal plausibility. To quantify the gap between ideal-
ized and executable settings, we define a reported
tool-performance ceiling Um for each automatic
metric m by substituting the officially reported per-
formance of the relevant underlying task-specific
tool (Feng et al., 2025; Isensee et al., 2021; Zheng

Observation 2: Tool-Level Competence Does
Not Reliably Translate into Final Success
Under the “Differentiated” setting, the Optimal
Tool Score (OTS, how closely selected tools rank
relative to available alternatives) and Milestone Hit
Rate (MHR, success in producing key intermediate
outputs) show that agents can often choose com-
petitive tools and complete important intermediate
steps. Nevertheless, response-level metrics remain
much lower: Target Hit Rate (THR, whether the
final tool aligns with the required answer format) is
limited, and BLEU, ROUGE, and F1 rarely exceed
0.35 (Figure 4a). This gap indicates that intermedi-
ate tool success is not sufficient for task completion.
The downstream bottleneck lies in target ground-
ing and response synthesis: agents must determine
which intermediate result should answer the query,
align the final output with the required response
format, and integrate tool outputs into a coherent
radiological response.
Observation 3: Tool-Set Perturbations Expose
Dependency and Grounding Bottlenecks
The “Redundant” and “Insufficient” settings re-
veal two complementary failure modes. With re-
dundant tools, the challenge shifts from choosing
one useful tool to maintaining correct data depen-
dencies among many similar candidates; the ECR

and PFS declines in Table 3 reveal I/O coupling
errors and downstream error propagation. With
insufficient tools, agents show stronger Unsolv-
ability Awareness Rate (recognizing that a task
is impossible) than Unsolvability Grounding Rate
(identifying what is missing): they may detect that
a task cannot be solved, but struggle to specify
whether the missing component is a tool category,
a modality-region capability, or a minimum func-
tional requirement (Figure 4b). Together, these per-
turbation tests show that robust radiology agents
require not only stronger LLMs, but also explicit
mechanisms for state tracking, dependency valida-
tion, target grounding, and failure-cause attribution.
3.2 Performance Enhancement
Prompting Strategies Mainly Improve Long-
Chain Coordination
Prompting strategies improve agent performance
by targeting the coordination bottlenecks identi-
fied above rather than simply changing the base
model. The integrated strategy increases task com-
pletion by 0.095 and 0.058 on simple and moder-
ate tasks, and by up to 48.2% on complex tasks
(Figure 5). Among individual strategies, few-shot
learning and multi-agent collaboration yield signif-
icant wins (p <0.05 ), whereas self-reflection and
back-propagation alone are less stable. Multi-agent
collaboration provides the most consistent gains by
improving long-chain monitoring and failure check-
ing, raising unsolvability awareness/grounding by
about 5%; back-propagation contributes smaller
but useful downstream correction gains (about 3%)
and, together with multi-agent collaboration, yields
the largest OTS improvements in the “Differenti-
ated” setting (0.021 and 0.029; Figure 5b).

with Task Complexity

anatomy–modality pair in config2, or the deficient

tion, the requested tool is simulated as available
so that the remaining tool-chain orchestration can
be evaluated. Across 220 QA pairs, the verified

trained medical tool.

We evaluate executable AutoTB in a multi-stage
real-environment setting: stability testing across
660 insufficient-tool scenarios, failure categoriza-
tion on 100 cases, and human clinical-utility as-
sessment on 30 real-world cases. Unlike the syn-
thetic protocol, M3Builder is actually invoked here:
it trains and validates the requested model, regis-
ters a successful tool in the executable pool, and
records build and downstream usage outcomes in
its logs. Tables 6–8 show that AutoTB substantially
improves solvability in regular and intermediate
scenarios, where trigger rates remain high (98.2%
and 92.7%), execution success is strong (94.5%
and 86.8%), and clinical utility is favorable (4.2
and 3.6). In complex scenarios, however, the trig-
ger rate falls to 78.6%, execution success drops to
65.4%, average build time increases to 29.4 min-
utes, and clinical utility declines to 2.1. Failure
analysis further shows that the main bottleneck is
operational precision rather than high-level aware-
ness: usage errors dominate (48%) while planning
errors are rare (9%), indicating that agents often
knowwhento trigger AutoTB but struggle to spec-
ify precise parameters, interfaces, and configura-
tions for complex tool generation. Thus, AutoTB
shifts failures from “missing capability” to “frag-
ile capability construction”, motivating schema-
guided tool synthesis, parameter validation, dry-run
execution checks, and tighter workflow integration.
4 Related Work
LLM-based Agents in Medicine.The evolution of
medical AI has transitioned from static classifiers
to dynamic LLM-based agents capable of multi-
step reasoning. Recent frameworks have adopted
collaborative multi-agent strategies (Tang et al.,
2024) or integrated clinical calculators, retrieved
guidelines, and specialized analysis tools (Jin et al.,
2024; Wang et al., 2025) to support more transpar-
ent decision making. In the imaging domain, archi-
tectures such as ChatCAD (Wang et al., 2024) use
LLMs to synthesize the outputs of specialized vi-
sion models. MedAgentBench (Jiang et al., 2025),
meanwhile, evaluates agents primarily through in-
teractive EHR tasks; it does not cover the multi-
modal, tool-integrated radiology workflows here.
Synthetic Data Generation and Validation.Par-
allel to agent development, the data scarcity–
privacy tension has motivated methods ranging
from masking and reconstructing de-identified
clinical notes (Ren et al., 2025) to prompting
LLMs to generate synthetic patient–physician con-
versations (Haider et al., 2025) and large-scale
medical image–text training data (Yilmaz et al.,
2025). Validation has accordingly expanded be-
yond lexical overlap: Sella et al. (2025) intro-
duced the Quality–Privacy Score (QPS) to combine
data-quality and privacy measures, while Adams
et al. (2025) systematically evaluated fidelity, util-
ity, and re-identification risks. In radiology, Sun
et al. (2025) generated synthetic chest-radiology
reports and controlled report errors for training
and evaluating error detectors. This line of work
remains largely text-centered, leaving structured
multimodal synthesis for tool-integrated radiology
workflows underexplored.
5 Conclusion
We introducedRadA-BenchPlat, a two-layer
benchmark for evaluating radiology agents across
planning, tool selection, execution, and failure
awareness under tool conditions. Our results show
that the main bottleneck lies in execution fidelity
rather than high-level planning, especially in long-
chain scenarios. By linking synthetic stress tests
with real-world execution, RadA-BenchPlat further
shows that practical progress depends on reliable
tools, aligned interfaces, and failure recovery in ad-
dition to stronger LLM cores. Prompting strategies
and AutoTB help narrow this gap, but persistent
usage errors suggest that agents often knowwhen
to act without reliably knowinghowto use the
required tools. Overall, these results define the ca-
pability boundaries of radiology agents and expose
the gap between idealized orchestration and deploy-
ment, motivating broader real-world validation.

Limitations
Our study combines synthetic stress testing with
real-world execution, but the connection between
these two layers remains incomplete. The syn-
thetic layer enables controlled diagnosis of plan-
ning, tool selection, and execution failures, yet
cannot fully capture institution-specific protocols,
tool behavior, data distributions, or workflow con-
straints in clinical environments. In addition, our
real-environment evaluation is based on 165 cases,
which limits the statistical power of cross-system
comparisons. Future work should narrow this
synthetic–real gap through confidence intervals,
multi-center validation, and adaptive benchmarks
whose simulated tool ecosystems better reflect
evolving radiology practice.
