Abstract
Clinical decision-making requires nuanced
reasoning over heterogeneous evidence and
traceable justifications. While recent LLM
multi-agent systems (MAS) show promise,
they largely optimise for outcome accuracy
while overlooking process-grounded reasoning
aligned with clinical standards. One critical
real-world case of this is gene–disease validity
curation, where experts must determine whether
a gene is causally implicated in a disease by
synthesising diverse biomedical evidence. We
introduce an agent-as-tool reinforcement learning
framework for this task with two objectives: (i)
process-level supervision to ensure reasoning
follows valid clinical pathways, and (ii) efficient
coordination via a hierarchical multi-agent
system. Our evaluation on the ClinGen dataset
shows that with outcome-only rewards, MAS
with a GRPO-trained Qwen3-4B supervisor agent
substantially improves final outcome accuracy
from 0.195 with a base model supervisor to
0.732, but results in poor process alignment
(0.392 F1). Conversely, with process + outcome
rewards, MAS with GRPO-trained supervisor
achieves higher outcome accuracy (0.750)
while significantly improving process fidelity to
0.520 F1. Our code is available at https:

crucial in real-world clinical decision-making.
2. Related Works
Biomedical Agentic Systems.Recent progress in LLM
agents has highlighted biomedicine and healthcare as natu-
ral application areas, where many tasks benefit from agentic
abilities such as literature grounding and integration with ex-
ternal data sources and tools. Systems such as MMedAgent
(Li et al., 2024), Biomni (Huang et al., 2025), AgentMD (Jin
et al., 2024), and MedAgent-Pro (Wang et al., 2025) demon-
strate the potential of LLM agents to support biomedical
reasoning through explicit planning, tool use, and workflow
execution across a wide range of biomedical tasks (Rose
et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). Studies on MAS further show-
case that coordination among specialised agents can yield
additional improvements, either through enhanced cognitive
roles (e.g., planner, critic, verifier) or from task-specific role
assignment (Wu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Yang et al.,
2025c; Liu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b). AI Hospital
(Fan et al., 2024) simulates doctor–patient interactions via
a multi-agent framework to benchmark multi-turn symp-
tom collection and diagnosis. MDAgents (Kim et al., 2024)
introduces a multi-agent framework that assigns solo or col-
laborative LLM structures based on medical task complexity,
improving knowledge-intensive benchmarks. Multi-agentic
benchmarks such as AgentClinic (Schmidgall et al., 2025)
and Agent Hospital (Li et al., 2025) facilitate medical agents
under simulated environments mirroring real-world clinical
scenarios.

LLMs has been explored as a way to further improve agen-
tic capabilities for biomedical tasks. While studies such as
SiriuS (Zhao et al., 2025), Med-R1 (Lai et al., 2025), and
DoctorAgent-RL (Feng et al., 2025) report gains on task
performances through RL, they largely rely on outcome-

Dataset ConstructionTraining, development, and eval-
uation data are derived from the ClinGen Gene–Disease
Validity Curation database (Rehm et al., 2015; Wright et al.,
2024). We filtered the curated records to identify entries
containing experimental evidence annotations linked to pub-
lications within the PubMed Central Open Access (PMC-
OA) subset. This process yielded a final corpus of 1,994
valid articles. We define each data instance as a case tu-
ple (g,d,D,E,y) , comprising the unique gene–disease pair
(g,d) , the set of associated open-access articlesD, the aggre-
gate evidence annotationsE, and the final validity classifica-
tiony. ClinGen records are organised into Gene Curation
Expert Panels (GCEPs), each chaired by specialists in spe-
cific clinical domains (e.g., theHereditary Cardiovascular
Diseasepanel). While all 58 active panels adhere to the SOP,
the specific literature distributions and annotation nuances
vary by domain. We exploit this organisational structure
by assigning entire panels exclusively to the training, de-
velopment, or test splits. The held-out panel split enables
evaluation of generalisation, as the model must transfer rea-
soning patterns to evidence curated by unseen expert groups
and across novel clinical specialties.
4. Process-Supervised Multi-Agent RL
We frame the gene–disease validity curation task as a hierar-
chical MAS, in which aSupervisor Agentπθ orchestrates the
reasoning process by invoking a set ofK= 6 specialised
sub-agentsA={a 1,...,a K}. Each sub-agent specialises
in a single evidence category (e.g.,Model Systems,Rescue,
Expression) via prompt-based role assignment. When in-
voked, a sub-agent evaluates a single article for evidence of
that type.

4.2. Reward Design
We trainπθ to predict the correct validity class while in-
voking specialised sub-agents to ground decisions in evi-
dence categories defined by the SOP. We optimise this with
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) using a rewardR that combines
outcome reward with tool call-level process reward.
Outcome Reward (Rout).Outcome reward evaluates the
correctness of the final validity classificationy. Given the
ordinal nature of the ClinGen scale (Definitive> Strong>
Moderate> Limited> No Known Disease Relationship),
we map the label setY to integer ranks rank(·)∈{0,...,4}
(where 0 = No Known Disease Relationshipand 4 = Defini-
tive). We employ a centered distance-based reward:
Rout(y,y∗) =σ·(1−α·|rank(y)−rank(y ∗)|),(6)
whereα= 0.5 centers the base metric to [−1,+1] (penal-
ising catastrophic errors while rewarding exact matches),
andσ= 4.0 scales the final reward signal. This results in a
value range ofRout∈[−4,+4] , incentivising the model to
predict labels that areordinallyclose to the ground truth.

Sub-Agent Supervised Fine-Tuning.A key difference
between the single-agent and multi-agent settings lies in
the granularity of the process reward. In the single-agent
setup, evidence subtypes are predicted directly, whereas the
supervisor in the MAS is trained only to route queries to
broader agent categories. We additionally evaluate the ef-
fect of training both the supervisor and sub-agents. To this
end, we train a shared sub-agent LLM via supervised fine-
tuning (SFT) on high-quality traces generated by DeepSeek-
V3.2 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) to fulfill all agent roles.
Although all sub-agents share the same model weights, each
invocation uses a role-specific prompt and constrains the
output space to the evidence subtypes relevant to that cate-
gory.
4.4. Experimental Settings
We implement all RL and SFT experiments using the veRL
framework (Sheng et al., 2025). To support tool use and
multi-agentic topology, both single- and multi-agent settings
are implemented using LangGraph, leveraging veRL’s inte-
gration to enable graph-structured agentic workflows. We
use Qwen3 models (Yang et al., 2025a) at three scales (1.7B,
4B, and 8B) for our experiments. All models support native
tool use and strong instruction-following behaviour. Exper-
iments with Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B were conducted
on a compute node with four NVIDIA H100 (80GB) GPUs,
while experiments with Qwen3-8B used a node with four
NVIDIA H200 (141GB) GPUs.Training Configuration.
All GRPO training utilises multi-turn rollouts with a global
batch size of 16 and 8 responses per query, 5 epochs. We
set the sampling temperature to 0.8 and remove KL regular-
isation. For SFT, we used a micro-batch size of 1, a global
batch size of 4, and a maximum sequence length of 32,600
tokens over 4 epochs. See Appendix D for full training
details.

5.1. GRPO with Outcome-Only Reward

5.2. GRPO with Hybrid Reward
GRPO with a hybrid reward combines outcome and process-
level signals to encourage strong outcome accuracy while
maintaining procedural validity. We observe that this hybrid
reward consistently improves process metrics while main-
taining high outcome accuracy (Figure 4). For example,
in the multi-agent Qwen3-4B setting, Agent Call accuracy
increases from 0.105 (base) and 0.082 (outcome-only) to
0.455, while Evidence F1 improves from 0.417 to 0.520,
with outcome accuracy remaining stable at 0.732. Similar
trends are observed for Qwen3-8B, where Agent Call F1
improves from 0.436 (base) to 0.611 and Evidence F1 from
0.469 to 0.542. Reward trajectories also reflect that hybrid
training improves both reward components (Figure 3).
Notably, across two agent configurations and three model
sizes, training with hybrid reward improves outcome accu-
racy beyond outcome-only training in four of six settings.
In the multi-agent setting, as Figure 5 illustrates, subtype-
level Evidence Accuracy and F1 improve substantially de-
spite the process reward being applied only at the agent-call
level. This suggests that improved high-level routing places
sub-agents in more relevant contexts, indirectly enabling
stronger fine-grained evidence prediction without explicit
subtype-level reward.

5.4. Case Studies
To examine how the single-agent and multi-agent formula-
tions perform under different levels of problem complexity,
we present two representative case studies. We select cases
where a single article is associated with the gene–disease
pair for simplicity. Full reasoning traces from both the
single-agent and multi-agent settings for selected cases are
provided in the Appendix B.
(1) POLR1D — Treacher Collins syndrome 2 (PMID:
27448281).
In this example, both systems recovered the relevantModel
Systemsevidence and assigned the correct validity classi-
fication (Definitive). The evidence is clear in the abstract,
well-aligned with the target disease, and does not require
weighing multiple categories. In such low-complexity set-
tings, the single-agent approach can directly determine both
the outcome and the process alignment.
(2) OCRL — Oculocerebrorenal syndrome (PMID:
22210625).
In contrast, this case involves multiple relevant evidence
types (Model SystemsandRescue). While both systems still
converged on the correct validity classification (Definitive),
the single-agent recovered only one of the two evidence
categories. By delegating to specialised sub-agents, MAS
identified an additional evidence type and produced a more
complete evidence profile. This suggests that when the
evidence signal is ambiguous or not directly stated in the
abstract, assigning sub-agents to focus on specific evidence
categories can improve process alignment.
Across both cases, the final validity classification is the same
due to the strong presence ofModel Systemsevidence. How-
ever, the divergence in evidence completeness in the second
selected case indicates that the multi-agent approach scales
more naturally to scenarios involving multiple experimental
evidence types.
6. Discussion
InterpretabilityAlthough our evaluation focuses on out-
come and process-level prediction performance, a crucial
advantage of MAS lies in its ability to produce structured,
evidence-specific intermediate outputs. These intermediate
rationales are indispensable for real-world clinical deploy-
ment. While a single agent can be prompted to produce both
the final validity class and supporting evidence, its reason-
ing is not easily decomposable by evidence type, limiting
interpretability at the level required for clinical curation.
By contrast, MAS yields structured outputs for each agent,
including explanation field that mirrors how clinicians
record natural-language evidence summaries. Moreover,
MAS allows reasoning traces to be inspected at the level
of individual evidence categories. This is crucial in clinical
curation settings, where evidence assessments accumulate
over time as new studies are published, and where interme-
diate evidence annotations are essential both for arriving at
a final validity class and for enabling clinicians to revisit
past decisions and integrate emerging evidence (Andersen
et al., 2025).
7. Conclusion
In this work, we introduce a process-supervised multi-agent
RL framework for gene–disease validity curation. Our ap-
proach trains a supervisor to coordinate specialised evidence
agents using GRPO with a hybrid reward that combines
outcome correctness and process-aligned agent invocation.
This hybrid approach substantially improves process fidelity
while maintaining strong outcome accuracy. We show that
comparable gains can be achieved by training only the su-
pervisor without updating sub-agent parameters, reducing
the computational overhead of multi-agent learning while
preserving the benefits of structured coordination. Although
a single-agent pipeline achieves higher quantitative perfor-
mance, the MAS produces evidence-specific intermediate
outputs that better reflect clinical curation practice. To-
gether, our study proposes a multi-agent solution to the
gene–disease validity curation task and positions multi-
agent RL as a promising direction for a broader class of
SOP-governed clinical tasks (Hunter et al., 2016; Thaxton
et al., 2022) that have received limited attention in prior
LLM studies.

Impact Statement
This work aims to improve the reliability of LLM-based de-
cision support in healthcare by aligning training objectives
with the procedural requirements of a real-world clinical
task. We introduce a multi-agent reinforcement learning
framework for gene–disease validity curation, a real-world
clinical task that underpins rare disease diagnosis and di-
rectly impacts both clinicians and patients. This task exem-
plifies a broader class of clinical reasoning problems that
require transparent evaluation of heterogeneous evidence
under strict clinical SOPs. By incorporating process-aligned
rewards framed as agent invocation supervision, our ap-
proach improves process fidelity while producing structured,
auditable intermediate outputs. These evidence-specific ra-
tionales are a critical requirement for real-world deployment,
enabling clinicians to inspect, revise, and update decisions
as new literature emerges. The system is designed to assist
rather than replace clinicians’ curation efforts, and was de-
veloped in close collaboration with a clinician with directly
relevant domain expertise. Beyond gene–disease curation,
we view this work as a first step toward tackling a broader
class of SOP-governed clinical tasks, encouraging more
trustworthy and clinically grounded biomedical AI systems.
