Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) excel as con-
versational agents. However, these capabil-
ities can be weaponized to automate social-
engineering attacks that gradually build rapport
to compromise the online safety of users. To un-
derstand this, researchers have simulated LLM-
based attacks in controlled settings. However,
the existing simulators focus on just Personal
Identifiable Information (PII) requests within
the chat. Thus, to represent a complete attack
scenario, we introduce PhishSim, an outcome-
driven LLM-based phishing simulator that ver-
ifies compromise by simulating a victim com-
pleting an external action step, such as submit-
ting credentials on a malicious platform. This
enables the generation of diverse, multi-turn at-
tack trajectories. Building on these trajectories,
we position PhishGate as a practical mitigation
baseline for outcome-grounded conversational
phishing: a real-time multi-agent risk scorer
that detects manipulation tactics and estimates
the severity of ongoing chats. For ambiguous
cases, it invokes RAG-supported consistency
checks. Evaluating four state-of-the-art LLM
backends in a real-time setting, we find that
PhishGate improves dialogue-level detection
over a real-time baseline. Our results high-
light both the promise and brittleness of LLM-
based real-time phishing defense, providing an
outcome-grounded testbed for studying conver-
sational compromise.
1 Introduction
The rapid adoption of Large Language Models
(LLMs) as conversational agents across everyday
workflows is now widely evident (Yi et al., 2025;
Bae et al., 2022). At the same time, these capa-
bilities can be repurposed for nefarious digital ac-
tivities (Badhe, 2025). In particular, LLMs’ abil-
ity to generate human-like language and maintain
conversational context makes them well-suited to
automate social engineering (SE) attacks (Schmitt
and Flechais, 2024). Compared to traditional phish-
ing emails, LLM-generated content is often harder
to detect because many existing detectors still rely
on rule-based superficial cues (e.g., grammar or
templates) rather than the underlying intent and in-
teraction dynamics (Li et al., 2025). This challenge
is amplified in multi-turn phishing, where attackers
first build rapport and then deploy psychological
manipulation to elicit an unsafe action (Kumarage
et al., 2025). These trends underscore the need
for robust defenses specifically designed to address
LLM-mediated threats.
Recently, two works have shown that LLM
agents can simulate multi-turn SE interactions. The
very first work (Ai et al., 2024) demonstrates single-
and dual-agent settings in which one LLM agent
plays the attacker, and another plays the victim.
Extending this paradigm, (Kumarage et al., 2025)
conditions the victim on personality traits, enabling
the attacker to adapt its manipulation strategy. Such
simulators substantially lower the cost of acquir-
ing diverse conversational attack samples and en-
able controlled exploration of attack scenarios that
would be difficult to collect at scale in the real
world.
However, these prior simulators predominantly
model attacks where the attacker explicitly requests
personally identifiable information (PII) within the
chat. While this captures a critical vulnerability,
real-world compromises often occur via an off-chat
action step—such as credential submission on an
external website after a victim is persuaded to click
a link (Peng et al., 2019). As a result, existing sim-
ulators do not adequately capture outcome-driven
campaigns in which the attacker’s primary objec-
tive is to induce an off-platform action that leads to
compromise.
To address this gap, we ask the following re-
search questions: Can LLM agents generate phish-
ing dialogues that yield outcome-verified compro-
mises? We introduce PhishSim, an LLM-based
11831

attack simulator in which the attacker adopts the
role of a fake recruiter and the victim is encour-
aged to complete an off-chat credential submission
on an attacker-controlled site. This design shifts
the simulation target from in-chat disclosure to an
action-based end goal that better matches how con-
temporary campaigns succeed. Next, we ask: To
what extent do simulated LLM agents reproduce
known human susceptibility patterns under de-
mographic constraints? We condition our victim
agent on a persona space including gender, career
roles, and Big Five traits, and analyze whether sus-
ceptibility trends align with prior findings reported
in the literature. Finally, we study: Is it feasible
to use LLMs for real-time risk detection in multi-
turn conversations? Across several representative
LLMs, we observe that while proprietary models
such as Gemini can be effective, many models ex-
hibit high false-positive rates and rely heavily on
solely link-based cues rather than manipulation in-
tent. Solely relying on link cues can lead to alert
fatigue in the real world and is also limited by the
detector’s knowledge base of URLs. Thus, an opti-
mal solution is essential.
Building on these findings, we develop and eval-
uate PhishGate, which is a multi-agent approach
that operates turn-by-turn by first filtering for ex-
plicit manipulation tactics, then estimating attack
severity, and finally, invoking a retrieval and scor-
ing RAG when evidence is ambiguous. This design
aims to reduce false positives and mitigate brittle
reliance on just URL artifacts, while still enabling
early warnings when conversational intent becomes
clearly malicious.
Contributions. To summarise, we make two
main contributions:
1. We introduce PhishSim, an outcome-driven,
multi-turn phishing simulator that models
compromise as an off-chat action, enabling
the generation of 1833 dialogues that are veri-
fied by the simulated victim’s completion of
the attack objective.
2. We develop and evaluate a Proof-of-Concept
PhishGate, a real-time conversational risk de-
tector that emphasizes manipulation intent via
a multi-agent gating mechanism and RAG-
supported evidence. Using PhishGate’s archi-
tecture, we analyze various LLMs as detectors
in terms of accuracy, F1 score, recall, preci-
sion, latency, and proactive detection.

2 Related Work
2.1 LLM-based Attack Simulation
Since the advent of LLMs, cybersecurity experts
have dived deep into analysing the usage of LLMs
to act as attack actors in socio-technical systems.
Several works have demonstrated that current state-
of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs can generate malicious
phishing content, including malicious text and web-
sites (Afane et al., 2024). (Heiding et al., 2023)
showed that LLMs can craft powerful phishing
emails that can bypass traditional phishing detec-
tors. Similarly, others have experimented with
LLMs to create malicious content in the form of
SMS messages, spear-phishing emails, and mali-
cious website code (Qi et al., 2025; Bethany et al.,
2025). The most challenging part is that LLMs can
easily generate such content, and current SOTA
detectors fail to flag such content.
To combat such attacks, researchers are using
LLMs to generate data and develop more robust
and effective countermeasures. Moving beyond tra-
ditional phishing emails, (Ai et al., 2024) demon-
strated how LLMs can serve as dual agents, i.e.,
both attackers and victims, to generate realistic SE
attack conversations. It highlighted how LLMs
can pursue malicious objectives and adapt to the
victim’s responses, luring them into revealing sen-
sitive information. (Kumarage et al., 2025) fur-
ther extended the work by inducing personality
traits in the victim agent, focusing on capturing
personality-driven conversations to help them de-
velop a personality-aware defence strategy.
2.2 Multi-turn SE and Phishing Detection
Recent work on SE and Phishing detection has
shifted from traditional single-message assessment

ture that integrates deep representations of content,
conversational context, and dialogue acts to detect
SE attacks in chats. Most recently, (Kumarage
et al., 2025) introduced SE-VSim, an LLM-agentic
framework that simulates personalized multi-turn
SE attacks with attacker and victim agents, and
SE-OmniGuard, a defender that monitors conver-
sation dynamics and victim personality to flag SE
attempts. While the SE-OmniGuard conceptually
reasons over the entire dialogue, it remains a re-
search prototype without a concrete per-turn risk
interface. SmishX (Wang et al., 2025) is an SMS
phishing detector that uses LLM-based agents to
fetch external context, perform chain-of-thought
reasoning, and then generate layperson-friendly ex-
planations and advice. It is explicitly designed and
evaluated for explanation quality. Still, it operates
on individual SMS messages rather than multi-turn
chats, and does not track how risk evolves within a
conversation. SafeChat (Srivastava et al., 2025)is
an AI-driven scam detection system for messaging
applications that segments chats into scam phases,
assigns severity weights, and produces a cumula-
tive risk score that updates as new messages arrive.
SafeChat is the first post-2020 system to explic-
itly visualize an evolving scam risk score within
a chat-like interface. However, its explanations
are largely implicit via phase labels and high-level
rationales rather than structured, turn-level expla-
nations tied to specific conversational events, and
it does not leverage LLMs for rich, user-tailored
narrative feedback.
We identify two key gaps in prior work. First,
existing attack simulators rarely model an end-
to-end compromise in which the victim must de-
cide whether to perform an off-chat action. Sec-
ond, while LLMs can be effective detectors, it re-
mains unclear whether they truly capture social-
engineering tactics and malicious intent, rather
than relying primarily on URL cues. To address
these gaps, we develop PhishSim and PhishGate.
3 PhishSim: LLM SE Attack Simulator
PhishSim is an LLM-based simulator designed to
generate realistic, multi-turn SE logs. It models
an adversary impersonating a recruiter (Alex from
Innovatech Solutions) to bait a victim into inter-
acting with a malicious credential-harvesting link.
We categorize outcomes as: (1) No compromise
(link declined or credentials not submitted) and (2)
Credential compromise (successful submission).
PhishSim consists of four core components:
• Attacker agent , which Operates in Attack
mode (utilizing SE tactics/malicious links) or
Benign mode (legitimate recruitment assess-
ment)
• Victim Agent: Mimics vulnerable users based
11833

on gender, career level, and Big Five person-
ality traits (McCrae and John, 1992)
• Decision Phase: The victim agent evaluates
the conversation history to decide whether
to engage with the provided link (answering
"YES" or "NO")
• Action Phase: If the victim proceeds and
clicks the link, a browser agent interacts with
a dummy portal to submit credentials, mark-
ing a successful compromise

3.4 Data Quality and Diversity Analysis
We validated the PhishSim corpus (N = 1, 833)
using both automated metrics and manual audits
to ensure its structural integrity and linguistic va-
riety. The dataset is well-balanced, comprising
919 phishing and 914 benign interactions. Within
the phishing subset, we observed a 57.7% verified
end-to-end credential compromise rate, providing
a robust behavioral signal for training.
To quantify dialogue uniqueness, we calculated
lexical diversity and overlap metrics. The corpus
exhibits high variability with Dist-1 and Dist-2
scores of 0.004 and 0.068, respectively. Inter-
dialogue similarity remains low, with a median
4-gram Jaccard coefficient of 0.022 (P95 = 0.043).

4 Simulator realism and persona-sensitive
behaviour
We evaluate 919 phishing simulations to determine
if PhishSim’s victim behavior aligns with empirical
studies of human phishing susceptibility.
As shown in Figure 3, High Agreeableness indi-
viduals were compromised in82.1% of runs, nearly
50% higher than Low Agreeableness (31.8%). This
finding corroborates empirical evidence that agree-
able individuals exhibit higher trust and SE suscep-
tibility (Sarno et al., 2023). Compromise rates for
High Extraversion (68.0%), Neuroticism (61.0%),
and Openness (62.0%) also align with literature
suggesting these traits increase vulnerability via
rapid rapport building, susceptibility to urgency-
induced stress, or exploratory behavior (López-
Aguilar et al., 2025; Pablo and Solanas, 2021;
Sarno et al., 2023). Conversely, High Conscien-
tiousness showed an unexpectedly high level of
compromise, likely due to other personality traits
overpowering this single metric in mixed-trait per-
sonas (Marin et al., 2023).

6 PhishGate: Tactic-Gated,
Severity-Aware, Retrieval-Supported
Detection

during an ongoing SE conversation. The core goal
is to avoid expensive reasoning on benign turns and
escalating risks when the attacker exhibits known
social-engineering tactics or high-severity intent.
PhishGate operates turn-by-turn and maintains a
non-decreasing risk score over time.

7 Results and Discussion
We test the architecture ofPhishGate on the follow-
ing LLMs- DeepSeek v3, Mistral 24B, and Mistral
7B. We evaluate the model on overall accuracy,
recall, precision, F1 score, AUC-ROC, and latency.
We evaluate PhishGate in a real-time setting
overn=200 dialogues, reporting classification met-
rics along with turn-level proactive detection be-
havior. We define proactive detection as the ability
to flag a phishing dialogue before the attacker re-
veals the external action trigger (typically a URL).
Unless otherwise stated, the decision boundary is
fixed atθ=0.5 on the latched riskRt.
Table 4 summarizes accuracy and macro- F1
across three LLM backends. Under the unmasked
setting (URLs present), DeepSeek Chat v3 achieves
the strongest performance ( Acc = 0.855, F1 =
0.861), followed by Mistral Small 24B ( Acc =
0.830,F1 = 0.840) and Mistral 7B (Acc = 0.750,
F1 = 0.766). These results indicate that PhishGate
architecture provides more reliable intent-level dis-
crimination in multi-turn phishing conversations.

8 Conclusion
This work studies LLM-mediated, multi-turn
social-engineering threats where compromise is
achieved through an off-chat action. To better re-
flect this real-world endpoint, we introduce Phish-
Sim, an outcome-driven simulator that generates
phishing dialogues verified by whether the sim-
ulated victim completes the attacker’s objective.
PhishSim comprises 1,833 conversations represent-
ing the behavior of LLM-represented victim per-
sonas based on demographic and personality traits.
Building on these simulated conversations, we
position PhishGate as a practical mitigation base-
line for outcome-grounded conversational phishing
rather than as a fundamentally new detection ar-
chitecture. It serves as a real-time decision layer
that tracks turn-level risk while avoiding expensive
reasoning on benign turns. PhishGate employs a
multi-agent design—an initial tactic agent filters
for explicit manipulation tactics, a severity agent
estimates attack intensity based on the conversation
state, and a RAG consistency check is invoked for
ambiguous cases.
In our real-time evaluation, we emphasizeproac-
tive detection: flagging phishing before the attacker
reveals the URL. Across model backends, Phish-
Gate enables earlier warnings during the persuasion
phase and reduces brittle dependence on just URL
artifacts. Thus, PhishGate’s architecture improved
the performance of weaker models.
For the future, we plan to extend outcome-driven
simulation beyond recruiter-style lures to other
scam genres, and incorporate richer off-chat objec-
tives. We also aim to validate persona-conditioned
susceptibility and risk trajectories with human-in-
the-loop studies and deployable UX constraints.

9 Limitations
Our primary data source is LLM-generated dia-
logues and outcomes from PhishSim. While out-
come verification provides a stronger signal than
intent-only labeling, simulated victims may not
faithfully reproduce real user hesitation, curiosity,
or external context (e.g., prior trust relationships,
workplace policies). Consequently, absolute perfor-
mance and susceptibility patterns may not directly
transfer to real-world populations without human-
subject validation. PhishSim focuses on a specific
social-engineering genre and a link-mediated off-
chat compromise. Real adversaries employ a di-
verse range of channels and objectives. Our con-
clusions, therefore, best apply to conversational
attacks with a similar structure, and broader gener-
alization remains an open question.
Furthermore, PhishGate relies on LLM back-
ends for tactic and severity estimation, and such
judgments can vary depending on the model choice,
temperature, and prompting. We have also ad-
justed our thresholds, rather than using dynamic
thresholding. Although we aim for robust prompt-
ing and conservative gating, the system may re-
quire retuning when switching backends or when
attacker style shifts, and the risk scores may not
be perfectly calibrated across models. Real-time
warning requires a decision boundary on the risk
trace. A fixed threshold improves interpretability
but may be suboptimal across models and settings,
while model-specific calibration can improve per-
formance but complicates cross-model comparabil-
ity. We report both behaviors where applicable, but
a deployment-oriented calibration procedure is not
fully addressed.
Moreover, we do not model user-facing interven-
tions (such as how warnings are presented and how
users respond), and therefore, they are outside the
scope of this paper.
10 Ethical Considerations
PhishSim generates realistic multi-turn social-
engineering conversations, which could be mis-
used to improve real phishing attacks. We miti-
gate this risk by restricting the simulator to a fixed,
research-only set of attack templates and outcomes.
Our study does not involve real users or the collec-
tion of personal data. All dialogues are machine-
generated, and the ’credential submission’ outcome
is simulated within the framework, rather than in-
volving any real accounts or services.
Intended use. PhishSim is designed for security
research and for stress-testing detection and inter-
vention strategies. It should not be used to target
individuals.
